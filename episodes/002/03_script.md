## 1. Hook (0:00~0:15) — 모두가 믿는 전제
Before you pay another tuition bill for a computer science degree, look at where it ranks among 73 majors for getting that first job.
For twenty years the advice was simple. Learn to code, and you'll never be out of work.
`[KINETIC] "LEARN TO CODE" stamps in; a green "SAFE" tag clips onto it`

## 2. The flip + title (0:15~0:50)
The New York Fed tracks recent college graduates, aged 22 to 27, major by major. In its latest numbers, computer engineering has the second-highest unemployment rate of all 73 majors. Computer science is fourth. [#1, #3]
`[CHART] 73 horizontal bars sorted by unemployment, camera scrolls from the bottom; CE lands at #2, CS at #4, both highlighted`
That puts a new computer science grad, at 7 percent unemployed, behind art history at 6.7, philosophy at 5.1, and nutrition science at 4.5. [#1, #2]
`[CHART] five bars side by side: CS 7.0 · Art History 6.7 · Philosophy 5.1 · Nutrition 4.5 · All majors 4.2 (dashed line)`
So is computer science still worth it? The answer is more interesting than yes or no.
`[TITLE] IS COMPUTER SCIENCE STILL WORTH IT?`

## 3. Pillar 1 — It's not "tech" or "engineering" (0:50~1:50)
The first thing to notice is how narrow this is. It isn't every hard major. Electrical engineering grads, on the same campuses, doing much of the same math, had a 3.2 percent unemployment rate. Mechanical engineering, 4.4. [#4]
`[CHART] engineering family: Electrical 3.2 · Mechanical 4.4 · Computer Science 7.0 · Computer Engineering 7.8`
The overall rate for all recent grads was 4.2. So the trouble isn't engineering, and it isn't math. It's concentrated in the majors that feed entry-level software jobs. [#5]
`[KINETIC] "NOT ENGINEERING. NOT MATH." → "ENTRY-LEVEL SOFTWARE"`
Other number-heavy majors are spread out. Physics sits at 6.6 percent, information systems at 6.0, mathematics at 5.8. But finance, just as heavy on math, is at 2.8. Nursing, a demanding science major, is at 2.1. [#17, #9]
`[CHART] five bars: Physics 6.6 · Info Systems 6.0 · Math 5.8 · Finance 2.8 · Nursing 2.1, with CS 7.0 as a ghost bar for reference`
So hard math doesn't predict the trouble. Finance and nursing are proof of that. What sets computer science and computer engineering apart is where they usually lead: straight into entry-level software jobs.
`[KINETIC] "NOT THE MATH" → "THE FIRST JOB IT LEADS TO"`
One caution up front. These figures are for 2024, one year, and some majors have small samples. Treat the exact ranking as rough. The gap between software and the rest is the part to take seriously. [반론]
`[STRIP] "2024 data · one year · small samples"`

## 4. Pillar 2 — Why entry-level software (1:50~3:00)
Why would software be different? A Stanford team led by Erik Brynjolfsson looks at payroll records for workers aged 22 to 25. In the occupations most exposed to AI, which include software developers, employment for that age group was about 19 percent lower by June 2026, relative to young workers in less exposed jobs. A year earlier the gap was 15 percent. [#15]
`[CHART] gap meter fills 15% → 19%, labels "Jul 2025" → "Jun 2026"`
The same study finds the drop comes from fewer hires, not more layoffs, and it finds no evidence of widespread job loss across the whole economy. [#16]
`[KINETIC] "FEWER HIRES" grows · "MORE LAYOFFS" fades`
That fits what the New York Fed table shows: the first job is harder to get. But the authors call their findings patterns, not proof of cause. Remote work and interest rates were moving at the same time, as we covered in our last video.
`[STRIP] "patterns, not proof"`

## 5. Pillar 3 — The other half of the table (3:00~4:10)
Here is the part that usually gets left out. Look at what happens to the computer science grads who do get hired.
Underemployment means working a job that doesn't need a degree. For all recent grads, it's 39 percent. For art history, 45. For philosophy, 47. For computer science, 19. For computer engineering, 16. [#6]
`[CHART] underemployment bars, CS and CE far shorter than the rest`
And the pay. The typical early-career computer science grad earns 87 thousand dollars. Computer engineering, 90 thousand. All majors, 58. Art history, 45. By mid-career, computer science is at 120 thousand, against 87 for all majors. [#7, #8]
`[CHART] early-career pay bars; then a second row for mid-career`
So computer science didn't turn into a bad major. It turned into a high-risk, high-reward one. The first job is harder to land. The jobs that land pay well.
`[KINETIC] "BAD MAJOR" crossed out → "HIGH RISK · HIGH REWARD"`

## 6. Pillar 4 — The long run points the other way (4:10~4:55)
The government's long-range outlook is more optimistic. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts about 1.9 million jobs for software developers, testers and quality analysts, with a median salary of about 134 thousand dollars. It projects those jobs will grow 10 percent over the next decade, much faster than average. [#13, #14]
`[CHART] one bar growing 10%, stamped "PROJECTION · BLS 2025–35"`
That's a projection, not a promise. But it separates two questions parents often mix up. Is the career still there? The forecast says yes. Is the first job harder to get right now? The data says yes too.
`[KINETIC] two cards: "THE CAREER: STILL THERE" · "THE FIRST JOB: HARDER"`

## 7. Counterpoint and the wider frame (4:55~5:30) — 75~85% 지점
You could argue none of this matters, because a degree still beats no degree. That's true. Young workers without a degree had a 7.4 percent unemployment rate in September, worse than recent grads at 5.2. And grads earned about 60 thousand dollars a year in 2025, against 40 thousand for young high school grads. [#10, #11, #12]
`[CHART] three bars: No degree 7.4 · Recent grads 5.2 · All workers 4.0`
But that comparison is about whether to go to college. The question here is which major, and for the first job, the major now matters more than the old advice admitted.

## 8. Punchline chart (5:30~5:50)
`[CHART] scatter: x = new-grad unemployment, y = early-career pay, 73 dots. CS and CE sit top-right, alone — highest pay, highest risk. Nursing and Electrical Engineering sit top-left.`
Put the two numbers on one chart and computer science sits in a corner by itself: near the top for pay, near the top for risk. A few majors, like nursing and electrical engineering, pay well with far less risk. [#1, #4, #7, #9]

## 9. Close (5:50~6:05)
What the data can say is narrow: in 2024, the first job out of a software degree was harder to get than almost any other, and it paid better than almost any other. What it can't say yet is whether AI is the reason, or whether it lasts.
So if your kid is in computer science, the useful question isn't "should they switch?" It's "what's their plan for the first job?"
