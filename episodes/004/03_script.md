## 1. Hook

Forty-two percent of college students working toward a bachelor's degree have seriously thought about switching majors because of AI. Thirteen percent already have. [#25]

`[CHART] two torn bars: thought about switching 42% · already switched 13%`

And their parents are worried too. Seventy-one percent of American adults expect AI to mean fewer jobs over the next twenty years. [#28]

So which majors are actually safe from AI?

`[TITLE] WHICH MAJORS ARE AI-PROOF?`

## 2. The honest answer first

No major is AI-proof. Nobody's data can promise that. But this year, research has started to show where AI is already showing up in the job market for new graduates. And it points somewhere specific. Not mass layoffs. The front door. [#21, #23]

`[KINETIC] "AI-PROOF" crossed out → "WHERE IS IT SHOWING UP?"`

## 3. The front door

Start with Texas. The Dallas Fed followed graduates of Texas public universities by major, from 2021 to 2024. The majors most exposed to AI automation were computer science, computer engineering, and languages. The least exposed were nursing, education, and psychology. [#22]

`[CHART] two stacks of torn labels: MOST EXPOSED (CS · computer eng. · languages) vs LEAST EXPOSED (nursing · education · psychology)`

The more of a major's tasks AI could automate, the fewer of its graduates had jobs a year later. And first-year earnings in the more exposed majors fell about five percent, relative to the others. [#22]

Nationally, the Cleveland Fed found unemployment has risen about one percentage point more in the jobs most exposed to AI than in the least exposed. For workers aged twenty-three to thirty, the gap is about two points. [#23]

`[CHART] torn bars: unemployment gap, all ages ~1 pt · ages 23–30 ~2 pts`

Anthropic's own usage data shows a smaller version of the same thing: workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five are getting into AI-exposed jobs less often than before. For workers over twenty-five, there's no such drop. [#21]

And Stanford payroll data shows employment in the jobs most exposed to AI fell about eleven percent from late 2022 to mid-2026, while in the least exposed jobs it rose about ten. [#7]

`[CHART] two torn bars: Most AI-exposed −11% · Least exposed +10%`

So far, AI is changing who gets in, more than who gets pushed out.

## 4. Safe by one measure

So if you only look at who's getting hired, the safest majors look like education. In the New York Fed's 2024 data, special education grads had zero point seven percent unemployment. Elementary education, one point two. [#2]

`[CHART] ranked strips: Special ed 0.7 · Misc. education 1.1 · Elementary ed 1.2 · All majors 4.2`

But look at the pay. Special education grads start around forty-six thousand dollars. Elementary education, forty-five. The typical starting salary across all majors is fifty-eight thousand. [#2, #1]

Safe by one measure. Among the lowest paid by another.

## 5. Where AI helps least

Microsoft researchers measured how much of each job's work AI chatbots can actually help with. At the top: interpreters and translators, writers, customer service. At the bottom: phlebotomists, oral surgeons, hazardous materials removal. Work done with hands, on a body, in a place. [#18]

`[CHART] two columns of torn labels with scores: high (translators 0.49 · writers 0.45 · customer service 0.41) / low (phlebotomists 0.02 · oral surgeons 0.03)`

They also say a high score doesn't mean a job disappears. It means AI can help with more of the work. [#18] And it isn't just low-paid work. Across jobs, they found only a weak link between how much AI can help and what the job pays. [#19]

And healthcare, where so much of the work is hands-on, is where the government expects growth. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects U.S. jobs overall will grow about three and a half percent over the next ten years. Healthcare practitioners, eight percent. Office and administrative support, down four percent, and the Bureau names AI-powered automation as one reason. [#8]

`[CHART] torn bars: All jobs +3.5% · Healthcare practitioners +8.0% · Office & admin −4.0%`

The fastest-growing job in the country: nurse practitioner, up forty-one percent, with a median pay of about a hundred and thirty-two thousand dollars. [#9]

## 6. The sweet spot

So the useful list is the majors that do well on both counts. In the New York Fed data: below-average unemployment, and starting pay of seventy thousand or more. [#3]

`[CHART] scatter of torn dots: unemployment across, starting pay up; the low-left/high corner lights up`

Nursing: two point one percent unemployed, seventy thousand to start. And only about thirteen percent of nursing grads are underemployed, against thirty-nine percent overall. [#3, #4, #1]

Civil engineering: two point three percent, seventy-five thousand. Aerospace engineering: two point two percent, eighty-five thousand. Construction services: two point two percent, seventy-five thousand. Electrical engineering, finance, and economics make the list too. [#3]

`[CHART] the sweet-spot cards pinned in a row: Nursing · Civil · Aerospace · Construction · Electrical · Finance · Economics`

And the jobs these majors often lead to are projected to grow over the next ten years. Registered nurses, six percent. Civil engineers, six percent. Financial analysts, seven. [#10, #14, #17]

## 7. The license

Many of these jobs share something else: a license. Seventy-one percent of healthcare practitioners hold one. In computer and math jobs, six percent. [#24]

`[CHART] torn bars: license held — healthcare practitioners 71.3% · architecture & engineering 19.1% · computer & math 6.4%`

A civil engineer needs a license to sign off on public projects. [#14] An accountant needs a CPA license, which takes a hundred and fifty semester hours of school. [#16] AI can help draft the plans and the numbers. Someone licensed still signs. That's our reading, not something these tables measure.

And not every safe path is a four-year degree. Electricians are projected to grow nine percent, after a four- to five-year apprenticeship, and most states require a license. [#15]

## 8. The twist inside tech

Now computer science. Higher pay to start, at eighty-seven thousand, but seven percent unemployed. [#5]

But tech isn't one job. Software developers are still projected to grow ten percent, at a median of about a hundred and thirty-six thousand dollars. Computer programmers, the more routine coding job, are projected to shrink seven percent, and the Bureau names AI as a reason. [#11, #12]

`[CHART] two torn cards: Software developers +10% · $135,980 / Computer programmers −7%`

In Anthropic's data, AI is already being used for about seventy-five percent of a computer programmer's tasks. For about thirty percent of workers, like cooks, mechanics, and bartenders, it hasn't shown up at all. [#20]

It's not only tech. Graphic designers are projected to shrink two percent, with AI design tools named as a reason. [#13]

So the question isn't only the major. It's which work inside it.

## 9. Patterns, not proof

These are patterns, not proof. The exposure measures don't use the same definitions. Most of this data is about jobs, not majors. The major figures are one year, 2024, with small samples for some majors. [#1, #22]

`[KINETIC] "PATTERNS, NOT PROOF."`

And hiring hasn't collapsed. Employers surveyed this spring planned to hire about five and a half percent more new graduates than the year before. [#26] Just over a quarter of them said AI has cut the need for some entry-level tasks. [#27]

## 10. Close

So, which majors are AI-proof? None, honestly. But some majors are doing well on both counts that matter for a first job: getting hired, and getting paid. And many of them lead to work tied to a place, a license, or a patient. [#3, #24]

A better question than "is it AI-proof?" is this: does this major lead to work that AI helps with, or work that AI does? And what does it pay?
