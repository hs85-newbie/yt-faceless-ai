- 게이트: 대본 승인 → 바로 제작 → 업로드 직전 확인(스프린트 #30, 2026-11-21 사용자 결정)

## 1. Hook (0:00~0:15)

Right now, a new college graduate is more likely to be out of work than the average American worker. Five point two percent, against four point zero. [#1]

`[COLLAGE] two torn percentages pinned side by side; "RECENT GRADS 5.2%" in orange, "ALL WORKERS 4.0%" in ink`

And the jobs that used to start a career are the ones AI is getting good at. So is college still worth the money?

`[TITLE] IS COLLEGE STILL WORTH IT WITH AI?`

## 2. The comparison that actually matters (0:15~1:10)

That first number is real, but it answers the wrong question. A family deciding about college isn't choosing between a degree and an average worker. They're choosing between a degree and no degree.

`[COLLAGE] a fork in the road drawn on graph paper: "DEGREE" / "NO DEGREE"`

So put those side by side. In September, young workers without a degree had a seven point four percent unemployment rate. Recent college grads were at five point two. [#2, #1]

`[CHART] two bars clipped from the report: No degree 7.4 (orange) · Recent grads 5.2 (blue)`

And the pay gap is wider than the job gap. In 2025, grads aged 22 to 27 earned a median of about sixty thousand dollars a year. Young high school grads earned about forty thousand. That's one and a half times as much. [#3]

`[CHART] stacked paper bills: $60,000 vs $40,000, "1.5×" stamped`

On the two numbers that pay the bills, the degree still wins.

## 3. Where AI comes in (1:10~2:10)

The worry about AI is real, but it's narrower than the headlines. A Stanford team looked at payroll records for workers aged 22 to 25. In the jobs most exposed to AI, employment for that age group was about nineteen percent lower by June 2026, compared with young workers in less exposed jobs. A year earlier, the gap was fifteen percent. [#7]

`[COLLAGE] cut-out line from the Stanford paper; a widening gap marked 15% → 19%`

The same study finds the drop comes from fewer hires, not more layoffs. And it finds no evidence of widespread job loss across the economy. The authors call these patterns, not proof of cause. [#8]

`[KINETIC] "FEWER HIRES" stamps over "MORE LAYOFFS" (crossed out)`

So the part that's shrinking is the first rung. That matters for a new grad, but it's a hiring problem, not a sign that degrees stopped paying.

## 4. A degree isn't a job (2:10~3:15)

Here's the number that should worry parents more than AI. Among all recent grads, thirty-nine percent were underemployed in 2024, meaning they worked a job that doesn't need a degree. [#4]

`[COLLAGE] a diploma clipped to a coffee-shop apron; "39% UNDEREMPLOYED"`

And the average hides a huge spread by major. In 2024, special education grads had an unemployment rate under one percent. Nursing was at two point one. Finance, two point eight. Computer science, seven. [#5]

`[CHART] torn strips ranked: Special ed 0.7 · Nursing 2.1 · Finance 2.8 · All 4.2 (dashed) · CS 7.0`

Underemployment spreads out the same way. Computer science grads who land a job are underemployed nineteen percent of the time. Art history grads, forty-five. Philosophy, forty-seven. [#4]

`[CHART] same majors, underemployment: CS 19 · Art history 45 · Philosophy 47`

So "college" isn't one bet. It's dozens of different bets with very different odds.

## 5. The honest counterpoint (3:15~3:50)

You could read all this and decide the safe move is to skip college. The data doesn't support that. In the same tables, the no-degree path has the higher unemployment rate and the lower pay. [#2, #3]

`[COLLAGE] the fork from section 2 again, "NO DEGREE" side now holding both worse numbers`

What AI changes is the margin for error. When entry-level hiring tightens, the major and the plan for the first job matter more than they used to. [#7, #8]

## 6. Close (3:50~4:30)

So, is college still worth it with AI? On average, yes. A degree still means lower unemployment and about one and a half times the pay. [#2, #3]

But the average is doing a lot of work. The first rung is getting harder in jobs where AI does the task, and the payoff depends heavily on the major. [#5, #7]

`[COLLAGE] the hook's two percentages, now with a third note pinned below: "WHICH MAJOR? WHAT FIRST JOB?"`

So the question for your family isn't "college or not?" It's "which degree, and what's the plan for the first job?"
