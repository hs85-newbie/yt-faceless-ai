## 1. Hook

There's a job that typically needs only a high school diploma, and it pays more than the typical job that requires a bachelor's degree. Elevator installers and repairers: a median of about a hundred and ten thousand dollars a year. The typical job that requires a bachelor's: about ninety-six thousand. [#6, #1]

`[CHART] two torn cards: Elevator installers $109,910 (high school diploma) · Bachelor's-level jobs $96,480`

And parents are noticing. Thirty-five percent now say career and technical education is the best fit for high-achieving students. In 2019, it was thirteen percent. [#24]

So is trade school the smarter bet now?

`[TITLE] IS TRADE SCHOOL BETTER THAN COLLEGE NOW? — 썸네일 구간: 제목 문장 뒤 2.967초 정지`

## 2. One number can fool you

But one number can fool you. Elevator installers are a small field, with about nineteen hundred openings a year. [#6] And that comparison is about jobs, not schools. It doesn't tell you what a graduate of any one program will earn.

So let's look at the whole picture: pay, cost, and what AI is doing to both.

## 3. College still pays

Start with the big numbers. Workers with a bachelor's degree earn a median of about fifteen hundred and seventy-eight dollars a week. With a high school diploma, about nine hundred and sixty-six. Unemployment: two point eight percent, against four point three. [#2]

`[CHART] torn bars: weekly pay — bachelor's $1,578 · associate $1,135 · high school $966`

Across all jobs, the typical job that requires a bachelor's pays about ninety-six thousand dollars. A job that starts with a high school diploma, about forty-eight thousand. [#1]

On average, the degree still pays. And one of the weakest spots is in between: some college, no degree. Those workers earn about a thousand and sixty-two dollars a week, much closer to high school pay than to a bachelor's. [#2]

But the start is rougher than it used to be. Recent college grads are unemployed at five point two percent. And forty-two and a half percent are working jobs that don't require a degree. [#13] Their typical pay: about sixty thousand dollars. [#14]

`[CHART] two torn cards: 5.2% unemployed · 42.5% underemployed (recent grads, 22–27)`

## 4. The cost

Then there's the price. The average sticker price for a year at a public two-year college is about forty-two hundred dollars. A public four-year in-state, about twelve thousand three hundred. A private nonprofit four-year, about forty-six thousand. [#15] That's tuition and fees, before aid, and before housing.

`[CHART] torn bars: sticker tuition 2026–27 — 2-year public $4,210 · 4-year public $12,290 · 4-year private $46,350`

Americans now owe about one point six six trillion dollars in student loans. [#16]

## 5. The trades map

So what do the trades actually pay? Electricians: a median of about sixty-three thousand dollars, with jobs projected to grow nine percent over the next ten years, and about seventy-two thousand openings a year. Plumbers: about sixty-four thousand, growing seven percent. HVAC technicians: about sixty-one thousand, growing eleven percent. [#3, #4, #5]

`[CHART] trade cards pinned: Electricians $63,190 +9% · Plumbers $63,800 +7% · HVAC $61,010 +11%`

That's above the typical job that starts with a high school diploma, and below the typical bachelor's job. [#1] And the path in is different. Electricians usually start with a four- to five-year apprenticeship. [#25]

Some two-year paths go further. Dental hygienists need an associate degree, and earn a median of about ninety-eight thousand dollars. Respiratory therapists, about eighty-two thousand. [#10, #11]

`[CHART] two torn cards: Dental hygienists $98,100 (associate) · Respiratory therapists $82,280 (associate)`

And jobs that start with a certificate instead of a degree are projected to grow five point seven percent. Jobs that need a bachelor's, five point six. [#1]

## 6. Where AI comes in

Now the AI part. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects office and administrative support jobs will shrink four percent over the next decade, about seven hundred fifty thousand jobs, and it names AI-powered automation as one reason. [#12]

`[CHART] torn bars: Office & admin −4.0% · All jobs +3.5% · Solar installers +37% · Wind technicians +30%`

The fastest-growing jobs include solar installers, up thirty-seven percent, and wind turbine technicians, up thirty percent. But those are small fields, with about thirty-one thousand and twelve thousand workers. [#7, #8] And degree jobs are growing too. Data scientists are projected to grow about thirty-five percent. [#12]

Microsoft researchers found the jobs where AI chatbots help least are physical ones: forestry and conservation, plant operators, groundskeeping, home health aides. [#19]

And in Anthropic's data, the workers most exposed to AI earn more, and are more likely to hold a graduate degree. About thirty percent of workers, like cooks and motorcycle mechanics, show no AI use at all. [#20]

`[CHART] two torn cards: most-exposed quarter earns 47% more · 30% of workers: no AI use seen`

So far, AI exposure is highest further up the education ladder.

## 7. The honest counterpoint

But trades aren't a magic exit. Welders are projected to grow just two percent, slower than average. [#9] And the work can be dangerous. In 2024, more than a thousand construction and extraction workers died on the job. [#23]

`[CHART] torn card: 1,032 work deaths, construction & extraction, 2024`

The New York Fed also notes that part of the pay gap may come from differences between the people who choose each path, not only from the degree. [#26] And young workers getting into AI-exposed jobs less often is a small effect so far. [#21]

## 8. What families are doing

Families are already shifting. In fall 2025, enrollment in undergraduate certificate programs rose six point six percent. Mechanic and repair technology programs, up about ten percent. Two-year computer science programs, down almost six percent. [#18]

`[CHART] torn bars: certificate programs +6.6% · mechanic & repair +10.4% · 2-year computer science −5.8%`

And the share of parents who prefer their kid go to college fell from seventy-four percent in 2019 to fifty-eight percent. [#24]

## 9. Close

So, is trade school better than college now? For some kids, yes. For others, no. The data says the better question isn't college or trade school. It's which job.

An associate degree in dental hygiene can lead to a job that pays more than the typical bachelor's job. A four-year degree in the wrong field can leave you underemployed. [#10, #1, #13]

So ask four things about any path: What job does it lead to? How many openings are there? Can AI do that work, or just help with it? And what will it cost to get there?
